Naikan: The Japanese Method That Dissolves Depression

A 1940s Japanese practice that treats depression with honest accounting.

  • Three questions per relationship: what they did for you, what you did for them, what trouble you caused them.
  • Most people find their giving was smaller and their harm larger than memory suggested.
  • The victim narrative is expensive to maintain; Naikan dissolves it by restoring the context pain stripped out.
  • Kyushu University research found significant drops in depression and improved relationship quality.

Fear vs. Desire: How to Know Your Answer

A clean little heuristic for when you can't tell which voice is talking.

  • Hunting for reasons not to do it usually means fear is negotiating you out of the right choice.
  • Hunting for reasons to do it usually means desire is building a case against what you already know is wrong.
  • Fear talks you out of alignment; desire talks you into chaos.
  • The answer isn't how badly you want it — it's how honest you'll be about why you're still debating.

The Greek Technique That Banned Fear of Failure

An ancient pre-game ritual that modern neuroscience now calls exposure reconsolidation.

  • Greek athletes practiced prokatalēpsis: vividly imagining defeat the night before competing to drain its emotional charge.
  • The amygdala can't sustain fear for outcomes the conscious mind has already accepted — cortisol quiets, survival mode exits.
  • Modern research calls this exposure reconsolidation: rewiring fear by confronting, not avoiding, the feared outcome.
  • Chronic fear elevates cortisol and suppresses T-cells, degrading clarity, health, and flow.
